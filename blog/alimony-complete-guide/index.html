Alimony

The Complete Guide to Alimony in Florida

Everything Florida spouses need to know about alimony: the types available, how courts calculate need and ability to pay, the 2023 law changes, and how retirement or lump sum awards work.

By Leisa Wintz, Esq. · Family Matters Law Group, P.A.

Alimony — also called spousal support — is financial support paid by one spouse to the other after a divorce. In Florida, alimony isn't automatic and isn't based on gender: it's based on one spouse's demonstrated need and the other spouse's ability to pay. This guide covers what alimony is, the types available since Florida's 2023 reform, how courts decide amount and duration, and what happens when circumstances change later.

Is Alimony the Same as Spousal Support?

Yes. "Alimony" and "spousal support" refer to the same thing in Florida law — a court-ordered or agreed payment from one former spouse to the other, separate from child support and separate from the division of marital property.

How Florida Courts Decide Alimony

Every alimony determination starts with two questions:

  • Need — Does the requesting spouse have a genuine financial shortfall based on the standard of living established during the marriage?
  • Ability to pay — Can the other spouse afford to pay that amount without compromising their own reasonable needs?

A financial affidavit from each spouse lays out income, expenses, and monthly shortfall. Judges have discretion here — even when alimony guidelines suggest one number, a judge may adjust based on the specific facts of the case, and even an awarded amount may not fully close the requesting spouse's gap.

How Long Do You Have to Be Married to Get Alimony?

There's no fixed minimum length of marriage required to seek alimony in Florida, but marriage length heavily influences what type and how much a court will consider:

  • Short-term marriages (under 10 years) — alimony is less common and, when awarded, is typically shorter in duration.
  • Moderate-term marriages (10–20 years) — courts have more latitude to award longer-term support.
  • Long-term marriages (20+ years) — support can extend for a significant portion of the marriage's length, though permanent alimony is no longer available under current law (see below).

Types of Alimony Available in Florida

Following the 2023 reform, Florida recognizes four forms of alimony:

  • Temporary alimony — support paid while the divorce case is pending, ending when the final judgment is entered.
  • Bridge-the-gap alimony — short-term support (capped at 2 years) to help a spouse transition from married to single life.
  • Rehabilitative alimony — support tied to a specific, documented plan for the receiving spouse to become self-supporting (e.g., finishing a degree or certification).
  • Durational alimony — support for a set period, capped at 35% of the income difference between spouses and limited by the length of the marriage.
Permanent alimony was eliminated for petitions filed on or after July 1, 2023, except in truly exceptional circumstances. This is the single biggest change from Florida's old alimony law and it does not apply retroactively to already-finalized cases.

Lump Sum Alimony

Rather than ongoing monthly payments, a court can also order alimony as a one-time lump sum — usually to give the receiving spouse the resources to become self-supporting immediately. Florida courts can combine lump sum payments with any of the four alimony types above. As with any alimony award, the court must make clear, written findings on need and ability to pay; failing to do so is one of the more common reasons an alimony award gets reversed on appeal.

Alimony and Retirement

Florida Statute 61.14 gives courts a structured framework for reducing or terminating alimony when the paying spouse voluntarily retires. Courts weigh nine factors, including:

  • The payor's age and health
  • The type of work involved and the customary retirement age in that field
  • The financial needs and resources of both spouses
  • The impact of retirement income and benefits
  • The motivation behind the retirement decision

This framework exists to balance the retiring spouse's right to eventually stop working against the receiving spouse's continued need for support.

Modifying Alimony Later

Alimony (other than durational, within certain limits) can generally be modified if there's a substantial, material, and involuntary change in circumstances — a job loss, disability, retirement, or a significant change in the receiving spouse's needs. This is a separate process from the original divorce and requires its own petition.
